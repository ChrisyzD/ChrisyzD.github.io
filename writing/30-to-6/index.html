30 to 6

On refinement, revolution, and the week the Cook era ended.

Chris Dai · September 2026 · ~4 min

Tim Cook handed Apple to John Ternus on the first of September. Fifteen years as CEO, twenty-eight at the company, and every retrospective I scrolled past this week went straight for the era — the trillion-dollar this, the services that. Fair enough. But the number rattling around my head is from before the era even started, and it is much smaller than any of those. Thirty.

When Cook showed up in March 1998, Apple was sitting on roughly thirty days of inventory. He was an industrial engineer out of Auburn who had spent years in the parts of IBM and Compaq that nobody writes books about, and he looked at warehouses of unsold Macs about the way an accountant looks at a fire. Thirty days became six within months. Eventually around two. He called inventory “fundamentally evil,” and the bit everyone quotes is the dairy one — manage it like milk, roughly, because once you are past the freshness date you no longer have inventory, you have a problem.

I have a soft spot for the pre-fame chapter of anybody's career, so this is where I get stuck.

On paper, 30 to 6 is refinement. Same job, done better, measured in the same unit. Except nobody refines their way from thirty to six. He didn't run the warehouses harder, he closed them — shut factories, handed production to contract manufacturers, tore up forecasting and rebuilt it from nothing. The number reads like an optimization. The move was a replacement.

Refinement and revolution. I am leaning on both words on purpose, and the shared R is doing real work: they start with the same letter and they get mistaken for each other constantly, usually by someone who has only ever done one of them.

Refinement has a curve and the curve is mean. Thirty to six costs a fortune. Six to five costs more. Every squeezed day costs more than the day before it, and diminishing returns are not a footnote to improvement work, they are the entire plot. Everybody doing this eventually reaches the flat part. Two honest options there: sit on the plateau, or change the system so the curve resets. The second one is revolution, and it is the only reason the first one ever gets affordable again.

Which is where I think the AI conversation is being read wrong, and yes, I am about to flatten a lot of things into one shape. Most of us, most days, work on 6-to-5 problems. Real, necessary, incremental, no shame in any of it. The panic assumes AI is coming for the 6-to-5 and that we are finished. I read it closer to the opposite. What actually changes is who gets to attempt a 30-to-6. That kind of move used to require a Tim Cook — a mandate, a decade, and a board that wouldn't blink. I watched a research workflow drop from two hours to five minutes because someone (fine: me) encoded the thing once and let a model run it. That is not a 6-to-5. That is the shape of a 30-to-6, executed by an intern. Am I compressing years of supply chain work into an afternoon script? Obviously. The shape holds anyway.

For whatever it says about me: I am drawn to the people who make boring things move. Nobody demos logistics. Cook never got a standing ovation for a supply chain. But 30-to-6 is the reason everything else in that era got to happen, because margin is what funds the machine. Given the choice I would rather build the measurement and the pipework that resets somebody's curve than add another decimal to a plateau.

So no, I don't find the era ending sad, and I don't think AI ends work. Plateaus end. Curves reset. The refiners who pick up the new leverage get a turn at being revolutionaries — and then, if it goes well, somebody new sits down and stares at their thirty.
