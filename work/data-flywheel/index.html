LLM data flywheel

Training data for a multi-model in-vehicle voice assistant. Instead of generating more data everywhere, measure first — then regenerate only where the failures are.

My scope

I led the data flywheel end to end for the multimedia domain of a five-person capstone: the user simulator, the rollout and judging harness, the deterministic governance layer, and the targeted regeneration. Why measuring comes first

When an assistant is weak, the reflex is to generate more training data. That scales cost linearly and quality barely at all, because most of the new data lands where the model was already fine.

The alternative is to measure first and let the measurement aim the spending. Simulate users, roll the assistant out against them, score the rollouts, and then read the error breakdown rather than the score — because the breakdown is what tells you which functions to regenerate against.

What I built

  • A user simulator with 41 persona archetypes and explicit state control, so a conversation has a goal and a memory rather than drifting.
  • Retrieval-grounded rollouts: candidate tools retrieved at top-K = 20 and grounded into the conversation, so the assistant is answering against a real tool surface instead of an imagined one.
  • An LLM-judge harness running on a GPU cluster, plus a deterministic governance layer — hard rules that pass or fail without a model's opinion — in front of it.
  • A four-quadrant corpus: positive and negative examples crossed with single-turn and multi-turn, so the fine-tune sees refusals and recoveries, not only successes.
  • Targeted regeneration against the 11 highest-error functions the judge's breakdown exposed, rather than uniform generation.

Boundary

This is an undergraduate capstone, not production, and it is a five-person one: I led the data flywheel end to end for the multimedia domain, a teammate owned the model-routing pillar, and the three numbers at the top of this page are end-to-end system numbers with my corpus as one input into them. Every one of them is also scored by an LLM judge, which is exactly the thing I am least comfortable with. Both of those are picked apart below.

Where the numbers come from

72.8 → 82.7% and 39.9 → 59.9% are the system's before/after on the same evaluation set, scored by the LLM judge described above. Cut to one decimal and never rounded up: the measured figures are 72.80, 82.72, 39.93 and 59.95, and the Experience entry carries them at full precision.

17,200 is a count of dialogues that scored reward = 1. The denominator it belongs to — how many were generated to get there — is the number I cannot put on this page, and the one I would put on the wall next time.

Further thoughts

“I led the data flywheel end to end” is a phrase with two edges on it, and both are worth saying out loud. It is a domain and not the project: multimedia, one of three, on a five-person team whose routing pillar was somebody else's. And what it covers is the corpus and the gates around it — the numbers above are the system's, and fine-tuning on my data is what moved them, which is a different sentence from the one where I ran the training. When I describe this work I try to keep that order — here is what the system did, here is the part of it that was mine — rather than letting the two blur together, because the blurring is effortless and one-directional and always flatters me.

The first check I would run is about the judge. reward = 1 is the judge's verdict, the corpus was filtered on that verdict, and the improvement afterwards was scored by a judge rather than by a person — so the judge is grading work the judge helped produce. Some fraction of 72.8 → 82.7 is the assistant getting better; some fraction may be the assistant learning what a judge likes; I cannot give you the split.

What would settle it is a held-out slice scored by hand, or scored by a judge from a different model family. I cannot point you at one. Until it exists the number is a strong internal signal and a weak external claim, and I would rather say that than let it be read as the second thing.

The second check is a denominator. 17,200 is a numerator I can quote; the generated-and-discarded count that belongs beside it is not something I can put on this page. That yield is the interesting quantity — it is what separates a simulator that produces usable conversations from a governance layer doing all the filtering downstream of a noisy one. It is why this figure stays a count of what exists rather than a rate of anything, and it is the first number I would put on the wall next time.

The two checks I would run first
  1. Is the judge grading its own work? The corpus was filtered on reward = 1 and the improvement was scored by a judge of the same kind. What would settle it: a held-out slice scored by hand, or by a judge from a different model family. I cannot point you at one.
  2. What is the denominator? 17,200 is a numerator. The generated-and-discarded count beside it is the yield, and the yield is what separates a simulator that produces usable conversations from a governance layer filtering a noisy one.
