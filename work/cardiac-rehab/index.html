Cardiac rehabilitation wearable study

Cardiac rehab is prescribed as a fixed 36-session protocol, the same length for everyone. The question was whether wearable step data can say when a given patient has actually stopped improving.

Why wearables make this answerable

Thirty-six sessions is a protocol. Some patients plateau well before it ends and are occupying a slot they no longer benefit from; others are still improving when they are discharged. Wearables record what actually happened between sessions, every day, for months — which makes “has this person stopped improving?” an empirical question for the first time.

Most of the work was not modelling. It was getting from raw device exports to a per-patient daily timeline anyone could reason about, and then deciding — explicitly, in writing — what “stopped improving” would be allowed to mean.

What I built

Four stages, each one a set of decisions rather than a library call:

Cleaning
DateTime standardization · type unification · missing-value marking · exercise-time capping (> 300 min) · step-count anomaly detection. Reconciled 400K+ raw step records from multiple sources onto one per-patient daily timeline.
Features
2-week / 4-week moving averages · week-over-week change rate · trend slope · coefficient of variation · stable-week count
Plateau detection
v1: change rate < 20%, CV < 0.30, duration ≥ 3 weeks → v2: minimum wait 8 weeks, moving-average slope < 200, change rate < 25%, multi-metric → v3: a Kalman filter over the same signal, so the plateau call comes from an estimated trend rather than from thresholds I chose. The thresholds are what produced the numbers on this page; the filter is where the method went next.
Phenotyping
PCA to compress the correlated temporal features, then K-means (k = 2) over the reduced space: higher-baseline / stable-progression / later-plateau vs lower-baseline / slower-progress / earlier-plateau
Reading the version numbers

The jump from v1 to v2 is the row to read, because you can read the diff. v1 was willing to call a plateau after three weeks of low change and low variability; adding a minimum wait of eight weeks and a slope condition is, structurally, an admission that v1 could call one too early.

Both versions stay in the table rather than being replaced, so the change is auditable — and both are on the poster figure below, where you can see what the admission cost.

Both figures are excerpts from one artifact: Dai, Liu, Lavieri, Moosavi & Golbus, Patient-Tailored Cardiac Rehab Discharge Policies via Operations Research Methods, poster presented at the U-M SURE Symposium, 2025. Results & boundary

  • The three numbers at the top of this page are the result: 400K+ raw step records reconciled onto one per-patient daily timeline, 247 → 191 patients once the cleaning and missing-value rules are applied, and ~68% of that cohort with suboptimal discharge timing against the standard 36-session model.
  • The cleaning rules and the plateau definition are both mine, and both are stated on this page rather than left to a methods section nobody opens — because either one of them, changed, changes that third number.

Further thoughts

Three defensible definitions of “stable recovery” move the median plateau day by roughly a factor of two. That box plot is the most useful thing here and it is also the thing that undercuts the headline, because any single number I report inherits whichever definition I picked. The correct response is a sensitivity analysis — sweep the thresholds, report a range instead of a point — and I have not run one. So “~68% had suboptimal discharge timing” is one defensible reading of the data.

There is a second circularity underneath the cleaning, and it took me a while to see it. Wearable step series are full of gaps, and the standard repairs — forward fill, moving averages — flatten a series. A flattened series is exactly what a plateau detector is looking for.

Which means the imputation strategy can manufacture the finding I was searching for, and v1, which asked only for low change and low variability over three weeks, was the version most exposed to it. The minimum wait and the slope condition in v2 are a partial answer at best. The real answer is to re-run the detector on complete-case patients only and see whether the number survives; that is the experiment I owe this project.

On the word “suboptimal”

It is doing too much work: it merges patients discharged too early with patients discharged too late. Those are two different clinical problems with two different fixes, and reporting them as one percentage is a convenience of mine.

I write “~68%” rather than a precise figure for a related reason — the defensible numerators round to either side of 68, and a number I cannot reconstruct is a number I should round.
