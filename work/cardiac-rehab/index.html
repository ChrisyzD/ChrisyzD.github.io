Cardiac rehabilitation wearable study

Cardiac rehab is prescribed as a fixed 36-session protocol, the same length for everyone. The question was whether wearable step data can say when a given patient has actually stopped improving.

April 2025 – January 2026 · Research assistant (SURE summer program, then continued on the study), University of Michigan · Python

My scope. The data and feature side: assembling the analysis dataset, setting the cleaning rules, engineering the temporal features, and the phenotyping and plateau detection built on them. This is supervised research with named co-authors — the poster below is the group's work and I am one author on it, not its owner.

What & why

Thirty-six sessions is a protocol, not a measurement. Some patients plateau well before it ends and are occupying a slot they no longer benefit from; others are still improving when they are discharged. Wearables record what actually happened between sessions, every day, for months — which makes “has this person stopped improving?” an empirical question for the first time.

Most of the work was not modelling. It was getting from raw device exports to a per-patient daily timeline anyone could reason about, and then deciding — explicitly, in writing — what “stopped improving” would be allowed to mean.

What I built

Four stages, each one a set of decisions rather than a library call:

Cleaning
DateTime standardization · type unification · missing-value marking · exercise-time capping (> 300 min) · step-count anomaly detection. Reconciled 400K+ raw step records from multiple sources onto one per-patient daily timeline.
Features
2-week / 4-week moving averages · week-over-week change rate · trend slope · coefficient of variation · stable-week count
Plateau detection
v1: change rate < 20%, CV < 0.30, duration ≥ 3 weeks → v2: minimum wait 8 weeks, moving-average slope < 200, change rate < 25%, multi-metric. Later extended with a Kalman-filter approach.
Phenotyping
K-means (k = 2) over the feature set: higher-baseline / stable-progression / later-plateau vs lower-baseline / slower-progress / earlier-plateau

The jump from v1 to v2 is the part I would point at, because you can read the diff. v1 was willing to call a plateau after three weeks of low change and low variability; adding a minimum wait of eight weeks and a slope condition is, structurally, an admission that v1 could call one too early. Measure, find the ruler wrong, fix the ruler — then keep both versions visible so the change is auditable.

Results & boundary

  • 400K+ raw step records reconciled onto one per-patient daily timeline.
  • Cleaning and missing-value rules cut 247 enrolled patients to a 191-patient analysis cohort — the exclusion rules are part of the result, not a footnote to it.
  • ~68% of that cohort had suboptimal discharge timing against the standard 36-session model.
  • Boundary: a supervised research project, presented as a co-authored poster. No patient-level data appears on this page; both figures are cohort-level charts from the published poster.

Further thoughts

Working notes — first person, unfinished, and mine to revise. This is the project where I am least sure the finding would survive a determined attempt to break it.

The plateau figure is the strongest thing on this page and the most uncomfortable. If three defensible definitions of “stable recovery” move the median plateau day by roughly a factor of two, then any headline number I report inherits whichever definition I picked. The correct response is a sensitivity analysis — sweep the thresholds, report a range instead of a point — and I have not run one. So “~68% had suboptimal discharge timing” should be read as one defensible reading of the data, not as a measurement of the world.

There is a second circularity underneath the cleaning, and it took me a while to see it. Wearable step series are full of gaps, and the standard repairs — forward fill, moving averages — flatten a series. A flattened series is exactly what a plateau detector is looking for. Which means the imputation strategy can manufacture the finding I was searching for, and v1, which asked only for low change and low variability over three weeks, was the version most exposed to it. The minimum wait and the slope condition in v2 are a partial answer at best. The real answer is to re-run the detector on complete-case patients only and see whether the number survives; that is the experiment I owe this project.

Two smaller debts. I write “~68%” rather than the precise figure I originally computed, because the exact numerator never came back out of the original notebook and the plausible candidates round to either side of 68 — a number I cannot reconstruct is a number I should round. And “suboptimal” is still doing too much work: it merges patients discharged too early with patients discharged too late. Those are two different clinical problems with two different fixes, and reporting them as one percentage is a convenience of mine, not a property of the data.
